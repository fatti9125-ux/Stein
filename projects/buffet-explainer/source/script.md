## SCRIPT

*[ ] = visuals or mascot cues. Everything else is narration.*

### Chapter 1: A $17 steak dinner with no limit

[FAST CUTS: sushi plates stacking up → shaky phone footage of chairs flying in a restaurant → a man holding a protest sign]

From a triathlete who got banned for eating a hundred plates of sushi, to forty people brawling inside a Golden Corral over steak, this is how all-you-can-eat buffets make money when people eat like *this*.

[B-roll: carving station, sirloin being sliced, steam rising off trays]

At a lot of Golden Corrals in America right now, about seventeen dollars gets you dinner. Not *a* dinner. All of it. Carved sirloin, fried chicken, shrimp, mac and cheese, a dessert bar, and roughly a hundred and fifty other things, for as long as you can keep walking back.

Nobody counts your plates. Nobody taps you on the shoulder on trip number five.

That makes the next part strange. For years, the biggest name in American buffets wasn't Golden Corral. It was Old Country Buffet, with more than 350 restaurants at its peak. Its parent company went bankrupt in 2008, then again in 2012, then *again* in 2016. By 2021, there wasn't a single Old Country Buffet left, and most of the furniture had been sold at auction.

[MASCOT: raises one eyebrow]

So the obvious answer, that buffets make money because most people don't eat that much, can't be the whole story. Both chains had the same kind of customers, the same unlimited plates and, I'm fairly confident, the same mashed potatoes. One is still opening new restaurants. The other one is a memory.

To work out why, we need a number buffets don't put on the sign: what it actually costs them to feed you.

### Chapter 2: What does it cost to feed you?

[On-screen: "38¢ of every $1 → food"]

According to the Wall Street Journal, about 38 cents of every dollar Golden Corral takes in goes on food. On a seventeen-dollar dinner, that's roughly six dollars and fifty cents of food per person.

Six-fifty. That's the budget, for a meal where the customer is allowed to try everything twice.

Of course, nobody eats an *average* meal. When The Hustle modeled a typical twenty-dollar buffet, it split customers into three types. The average eater cost the restaurant about $7.40 in food. Someone who mostly went for bread and potatoes cost about $4.70. And the person who walked past the salad and went straight back for more meat, let's call him Gary, cost about $16.90.

[Visual: 20 little diner icons, 19 grey, 1 red labelled "GARY"]

In that model, only about one diner in twenty eats like Gary. And the restaurant doesn't need to make money on Gary. It needs the other nineteen people to quietly cover him.

But every now and then, someone walks in who makes Gary look like he's on a diet.

[Photo/B-roll: sushi conveyor belt, stacks of colored plates]

In September 2018, a 30-year-old German triathlete and former bodybuilder called Jaroslav Bobrowski walked into a restaurant called Running Sushi in Landshut, Bavaria. It was all-you-can-eat for €15.90, about eighteen and a half dollars. Bobrowski follows a diet where he doesn't eat for 20 hours, and then eats until he's full.

That evening, "full" turned out to be around one hundred plates of sushi.

He wasn't taking one plate at a time either. The owner said he'd grab five or seven at once, leaving such big gaps on the conveyor belt that other customers asked whether the restaurant had started skimping on fish. So when Bobrowski went to pay, and even tried to leave a tip, the waiter refused the tip and the owner told him he was banned for good. In the owner's words, he ate for five people.

[MASCOT: slowly counts plates on fingers, gives up]

And Bobrowski isn't the only one. In 2012, a 6-foot-6, 350-pound man named Bill Wisth went to the Friday all-you-can-eat fish fry at Chuck's Place in Thiensville, Wisconsin. After twelve pieces of fish, the restaurant cut him off. So two days later, he came back with a picket sign, called it false advertising, and said he'd protest outside every Sunday until "all you can eat" meant all he could eat.

Both stories show the same thing. For a small restaurant, one extreme eater is a real cost. Notice who paid for it in the sushi story, though: it wasn't the sushi bill, it was the other customers staring at an empty conveyor belt.

To be fair, even an ordinary buffet runs on a thinner cushion than it sounds. Once you add staff, rent, electricity and everything else, The Hustle's model left the buffet with about one dollar of profit per customer. That's five percent. So buffets can't just *hope* most people eat less. They make sure of it, and it starts before you've picked up a plate.

### Chapter 3: Why are the rolls always first?

[B-roll: walking POV down a buffet line: salad, bread, potatoes, fried food, then the carving station at the very end]

Walk into almost any buffet and look at what's at the start of the line: salad, bread rolls, potatoes, something fried. The expensive meat is usually further in, and it's usually served by a person holding a knife and a fairly specific idea of what a portion looks like.

That layout isn't an accident. In one well-known study of buffet lines, about three-quarters of people took whatever was in the first tray, and two-thirds of everything people ate came from the first three. By the time you reach the steak, most of your plate is already taken, by a potato that costs the restaurant about thirty cents a serving.

The steak, by comparison, costs about $2.25 a serving at wholesale.

Golden Corral goes a step further. Its buffet is arranged in what it calls *modules*, which pair expensive items with cheap ones on purpose. The meat and seafood come with hush puppies and fries. The meatloaf comes with potatoes, gravy and green beans. You didn't choose that side dish. It was chosen for you, probably in a meeting with a spreadsheet.

[MASCOT: pushes glasses up, points at a spreadsheet]

Then there are the small details. The potatoes get bigger serving spoons. The meat gets smaller tongs. The plates are smaller than you'd expect, and the water glasses are bigger and keep getting refilled. None of this stops you from eating steak. It just makes steak a little more effort than everything around it.

And when steak is the one thing on the buffet that's rationed by design, people notice when it runs low.

[Blurred phone footage / news stills: overturned tables]

On January 29th, 2022, at a Golden Corral in Bensalem, Pennsylvania, an argument broke out in the line for steak. Depending on which witness you ask, someone cut in line, or the steak was running out, or both. Either way, it didn't stay an argument for long. Within minutes, around forty people were fighting in the middle of the restaurant.

But somehow, it got even worse. The video, which went viral, shows chairs flying, tables flipping, and at one point people swinging *high chairs*. An employee was hit by a table. And as things finally calmed down, one man can be heard saying, "All I wanted was some steak."

Luckily, no serious injuries were reported. But it's probably the clearest proof that the most valuable thing in a buffet isn't on the menu. It's the queue for the carving station.

[MASCOT: puts on a tiny helmet]

That's also why, when beef prices went up, Golden Corral added a bone-in fried pork chop. It costs them about 40 percent of what the sirloin does. Nobody is forced to pick it, but everyone who does saves the restaurant a little money and still feels like they got a treat.

You can see the result in the shopping list. According to The Atlantic, the company behind Old Country Buffet was serving about 47 million pounds of chicken a year, and about 6 million pounds of steak. That's nearly eight pounds of chicken for every pound of steak.

So the food is cheap and the steak is surrounded by potatoes. And so far, the only thing the buffet has charged you for is getting through the door.

### Chapter 4: Would you like a drink with that?

[On-screen: "Soda: ~12¢ → $2+"]

At most Golden Corrals, the drink isn't included. A soda or iced tea is usually somewhere around two and a half to three dollars extra. And fountain soda is one of the best deals in the restaurant business, for the restaurant. One estimate put the cost of a fill at about twelve cents. Sell that for two dollars and you've got a markup of well over one thousand percent.

On a seventeen-dollar dinner that contains six-fifty of food, the drink is almost pure profit stacked on top, and you'll probably buy it before you've seen a single tray.

Then there's the kids' deal. Right now, on Fridays, Saturdays and Sundays, children up to twelve can eat at Golden Corral for $2.50 with each adult buffet. On paper, that sounds like a terrible deal for the restaurant. In practice, a seven-year-old is not a serious threat to the sirloin supply. And a $2.50 child never arrives alone. They come with at least one adult paying full price, and usually a couple of drinks.

[Visual: receipt growing line by line: Adult buffet, Drink, Adult buffet, Drink, Kid $2.50]

So by now, the seventeen-dollar buffet has turned into seventeen dollars, a drink, a second adult, and a child who ate four chicken nuggets and a pudding cup.

Which brings us back to the real question. If the model works this well, why did so many other buffet chains go broke?

### Chapter 5: Why did the other buffets go bankrupt?

[B-roll / stills: closed buffet storefronts, "CLOSED" signs, empty dining rooms]

Here's the part that surprises people: it wasn't Gary, and it wasn't triathletes either.

Old Country Buffet's parent company took its final shape in 2006, when it bought Ryan's, another huge buffet chain, and borrowed heavily to do it. That debt led to its first bankruptcy in 2008. It filed again in 2012, and again in 2016.

In early 2016, it shut 74 restaurants in one go, then 92 more about a month later, often without warning its own employees first. That same year, the owner of Souplantation and Sweet Tomatoes, another big buffet chain, went bankrupt carrying around $175 million in debt.

[MASCOT: staring at an extremely long bill]

Debt is especially dangerous for a buffet because of how the business works. A normal restaurant cooks your food after you order it. A buffet cooks first and hopes people turn up. If the dining room is half empty at seven o'clock, the trays still have to be full and the turkey still has to be carved. Somewhere between 5 and 25 percent of any given dish can end up in the bin anyway.

So a buffet doesn't really lose money on big eaters. It loses money on empty chairs.

Then in 2020, a pandemic turned sharing serving tongs with strangers into the least appealing idea in America, and what was left of those chains didn't make it. By early 2021, every Old Country Buffet, HomeTown Buffet and Ryan's had closed.

The odd thing is that the most famous buffets in the world went through the same collapse, and they had never even tried to make money.

### Chapter 6: Why did Las Vegas give up on buffets?

[B-roll: Las Vegas Strip at night, old casino buffet signs]

For decades, the Las Vegas buffet was designed to lose money. Casino executives openly called them *loss leaders*. Back in the 1980s, gambling brought in about three-quarters of a casino's revenue, so a cheap all-you-can-eat dinner was just a way to get you through the door, fill you up fast and send you back to the tables.

That works as long as the tables are the business. But more and more visitors to Las Vegas don't gamble much, or at all. Once people were happy to pay full price for food, a giant room losing money on crab legs started to look a lot less clever.

Around the year 2000, there were roughly 35 buffets on the Las Vegas Strip. Today there are about seven. A lot of that space became food halls, which charge you separately for every single thing you eat. That is, if you think about it, the exact opposite of a buffet.

[MASCOT: shrugs]

Vegas buffets were built to lose money, and once casinos stopped needing them, they disappeared. Golden Corral doesn't have a casino quietly paying for the shrimp. The food has to pay for itself. So how does it manage that?

### Chapter 7: So how does $17 make money?

[On-screen: "6,000–6,500 customers per week, per restaurant"]

The answer is mostly scale, the kind a normal sit-down restaurant can't get. A typical Golden Corral serves somewhere around six to six and a half thousand customers a week. Its best newer restaurants average around $7.5 million a year in sales, and one in Puerto Rico recently became the first new location to pass $12 million.

This is exactly what Running Sushi in Bavaria and Chuck's Place in Wisconsin didn't have. When you serve a few hundred people a night, one Bobrowski empties the conveyor belt. When you serve six thousand a week, one Bobrowski is a rounding error.

That volume changes the kitchen too. A cook at a normal restaurant might manage about 25 customers an hour at best. A buffet cook preparing big trays can prep enough for around 200 people in the same time. You're not paying someone to cook *your* steak. You're paying a tiny share of someone cooking a very large amount of steak.

Golden Corral also forecasts demand in fifteen-minute increments, so it isn't cooking huge batches and leaving them under heat lamps. That's how you fight the waste problem that sank a lot of its competitors, and, hopefully, how you avoid running out of steak on a Saturday night in Pennsylvania.

And it counts on loyalty. Its most loyal guests come in around 70 times a year, more than once a week. At that point, it's not really a restaurant. It's a second kitchen with a chocolate pudding machine.

To be fair, none of this makes buffets a money printer. Golden Corral finished 2025 with 348 restaurants, three fewer than the year before. Beef is expensive, margins were thin to begin with, and its CEO recently said, "We are trying to at least tread water."

[Visual: callback montage: roll, pork chop, soda cup, $2.50 kid, calendar showing 70 visits]

So seventeen dollars isn't really the price of the food. It's a bet. A bet that you'll start with the rolls, that the meatloaf will bring its potatoes along, that you'll order a drink, bring a kid, and come back next Thursday. And most of us do every single one of those things.

Yes, Gary is still allowed to skip the bread, ignore the soda and eat four plates of sirloin. Bobrowski is still welcome too, just not in Landshut. The buffet just needs the other nineteen of us to really, really like potatoes.

[MASCOT: waves]

## Fact-check notes (read before recording)

- **Prices vary by location.** $16.99 is the commonly reported 2026 dinner price, and Golden Corral says it's trying to keep dinner under $20. Drinks are typically about $2.50–$3.
- **"38 cents of every dollar"** comes from the Wall Street Journal (via Fox News, Oct 2026). The $6.50 is our own rough math: $17 × 0.38.
- **$7.40 / $4.70 / $16.90, "1 in 20" and "$1 profit"** come from The Hustle's *hypothetical* model of a $20 buffet, not from Golden Corral's own data. Keep the attribution.
- **Bobrowski (2018):** widely reported by The Local, NPR and Fox News. He fasts for about 20 hours, the price was €15.90, and the owner said he took 5–7 plates at a time and that customers asked if they'd skimped on fish. Some outlets say "nearly 100 plates" rather than exactly 100, which is why the script says "around."
- **Bill Wisth (2012):** reported by UPI, CS Monitor and NBC. He was cut off after 12 pieces at Chuck's Place in Thiensville, WI, then picketed on Sundays.
- **Bensalem brawl (Jan 29, 2022):** around 40 people. The steak cause comes from witnesses and wasn't confirmed by Golden Corral, so the script says "depending on which witness you ask." The franchise said no serious injuries were reported, and KYW reported an employee was struck by a table. Use blurred stills or news screenshots, not the raw viral video, to avoid copyright and community-guideline issues.
- **"First tray" study:** widely cited, but one of its authors later had several papers retracted. For extra safety, change it to "studies of buffet lines suggest…" or drop the percentages.
- **Kids $2.50 deal:** Fridays to Sundays with an adult buffet, scheduled through the end of 2026. Check it's still running when you publish.
- **CEO quote** ("We are trying to at least tread water"): Lance Trenary, as reported in Oct 2026.
- The **chocolate pudding machine** is a joke. Swap in whatever dessert your B-roll shows.
